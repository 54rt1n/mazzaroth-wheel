# Working hypotheses to pursue

The inquiry is guided by the user's intuition. The purpose of these notes is to generate concrete examples, counterexamples and tests, rather than let received identifications determine the result in advance.

## Preserved geometry and star selection

Hypothesis: a name's radial and angular placement constrains which star or stellar group it represents.

The preserved circular astrolabes support the three-ring, twelve-month framework: Enlil inside, Anu middle, Ea outside. The present D3 calendar wheel follows that ordering. Its equally spaced markers are drawing choices, so they cannot serve as independent measurements of the ancient originals.

Next empirical test: digitize surviving original incised positions in Sm. 162 and K. 14943+; separate incised star symbols from textual labels and restored regions; fit candidate projections using secure identifications; test withheld names. Examine whether radius encodes continuous polar distance, broad sky bands, or another ordering. Keep alternative identifications available.

The actual-catalogue view already provides measured stellar positions and explicit polar geometry. Its center is defined as the ICRS north pole; Polaris is 0.7359° away at stellar epoch 2000. A recovered ancient center requires an independent fit and an ancient epoch, not recentering the modern chart on Polaris.

Evidence: Monroe (2022), https://doi.org/10.1177/00218286221110919; original objects https://cdli.earth/artifacts/254802 and https://cdli.earth/artifacts/401083. Reiner and Pingree (1981), pp. 3–4, record discrepancies between conventional identifications and simple declination bands; these are useful test cases.

## Twelve stations, 360 divisions, five additional days

Hypothesis: an ordered annual cycle of twelve thirty-part stations links the celestial wheel, the 360-part circle, and traditions treating five further days as outside the regular monthly order.

Supporting examples: Babylonian ideal years of twelve thirty-day months; the later zodiac of twelve thirty-degree signs; Egyptian 360+5 calendrical structure and its divine-birth narrative; Zoroastrian twelve thirty-day months with five Gathic days. De Jong explicitly presents calendar-to-zodiac analogy as a suggested historical explanation.

Tests and contrasts: distinguish early 360-day schemes from the later uniform zodiac; distinguish the Babylonian civil lunisolar calendar's extra month from Egyptian and Zoroastrian additional days; examine actual transmission evidence rather than infer a single common story from shared arithmetic. The ritual significance and naming of the additional days may supply particularly useful comparative material.

Sources: Teije de Jong (2019), §3, https://doi.org/10.1007/s00407-018-0216-0; John Steele (2013), https://www.sciamvs.org/files/SCIAMVS_14_003-039_Steele.pdf; Plutarch, *On Isis and Osiris* §12, https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Isis_and_Osiris*/A.html; Antonio Panaino, *Calendars i*, https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/calendars/calendars-i-pre-islamic-calendars/.

## Names and transmission

Keep three records distinct: a transmitted name and its slot, uses of the name in other texts, and a modern astronomical identification. For Dilbat, Šulpae, Salbatanu and Marduk, other-text planetary associations remain available as evidence, while the original referents in this list remain unresolved. Neither planetary nor fixed-star readings should be decided solely by the needs of the reconstruction.

Hebrew, Akkadian and Iranian correlations in the atlas offer points of comparison. A Median Magian transmission path remains an inquiry to pursue through dated texts, shared names, narrative structure and evidence of contact.
